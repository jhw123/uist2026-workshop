## 2. Related Work

### 2.1. Making Sense of Online Information

Building on theories of sensemaking as defined as developing a mental model of an information space in service of a user’s goals (Dervin, 1983; Klein et al., 2006; Russell et al., 1993), at a high level, a typical online sensemaking process involves first *reading and understanding* information from various data sources (e.g., online comparison articles, blog posts, video reviews, etc.), and then *collecting and organizing* such information to form a schema or representation (e.g., a comparison table, a decision tree, etc.) to interpret the space (Pirolli and Card, 2005; Chang et al., 2020).
Notably, there have been many research as well as commercial tools and systems developed to support this latter stage of sensemaking, i.e., collecting and organizing information. For example, tools like SearchPad (Bharat, 2000), Hunter Gather (schraefel et al., 2002), as well as commercial systems like the Evernote clipper (Evernote, [n. d.]), allow a user to capture entire pages or portions of web content, categorize them, and later assemble them into a coherent document or structure for their own sensemaking, decision making, or sharing and collaboration. Furthermore, to reduce the disruption to users’ overall workflow, prior work has also explored using lightweight interactions (Liu et al., 2022b; Chang et al., 2016a; Chen et al., 2023; Horvath et al., 2022) to streamline the process of collection and triaging information, or even automatically keeping track of content of interest on behalf of the user (Dontcheva et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2022a; Hogue and Karger, 2005; Chang et al., 2021).

However, an inherent assumption that these prior systems make is that the user has a well-developed mental model that will allow them to effectively grasp the content that they are about to collect using the system. But, in many circumstances, building sufficient context about the information space can be both time and effort-intensive, requiring individuals to iteratively read through and understand unfamiliar content by themselves without expert guidance or systematic strategy, as has been discussed in the literature (Nguyen et al., 2018; Spichtig et al., 2016) and confirmed in our formative study. Despite being feature-rich, aforementioned sensemaking tools generally fail to address the “cold start” issue during the initial reading and comprehension stage where users need help deciding “what is important to read” (Fok et al., 2023) and “how to most effectively read it” (August et al., 2023; Rachatasumrit et al., 2022; Head et al., 2022).
Discovering new and unseen content after reading can also be surprisingly difficult, as prior work revealed that a generic search query could return many redundant and near-duplicate documents (Pereira et al., 2006; Di Lucca et al., 2002; Poonkuzhali et al., 2011) despite the extensive use of duplicate detection algorithms in modern search engines (Plegas and Stamou, 2013). Therefore, in this work, we focus on supporting users’ reading and understanding of unfamiliar content in the first place.

### 2.2. Tool Support for Reading and Comprehension

One crucial way to address the “cold start” issue is to provide users with a summary of the information landscape. Previous research has investigated mechanisms that prompt domain experts to iteratively and collectively summarize a single document or discussion thread (Zhang et al., 2017; Zhang and Cranshaw, 2018; Gilmer et al., 2023). In addition, prior work has introduced tools and systems that facilitate finding, extracting, and summarizing relevant information from multiple documents and sources into cohesive themes and knowledge (Liu et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2019). However, these methods are dependent on the significant investment of time and effort from previous users and are contingent upon their availability, and may still result in summaries that are limited in scope (Paul and Morris, 2009; Dourish and Bellotti, 1992; Liu et al., 2021; Zhang and Cranshaw, 2018) or with a biased perspective (Flanagin and Metzger, 2000; Hoorn and Wijngaarden, 2010).

More recently, researchers have been experimenting with approaches to extract key information and build underlying knowledge structures automatically with machine learning (Das et al., 2021; Brody and Elhadad, 2010; Jin et al., 2009; Angelidis and Lapata, 2018; Fok et al., 2023), lexical and HTML patterns (Kong and Allan, 2014; Dontcheva et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2022a), or crowdsourcing (Chang et al., 2016b; Chilton et al., 2013; Hahn et al., 2016). However, many studies have demonstrated that these techniques can often produce structures that are incoherent and difficult for users to comprehend and contextualize (Chang et al., 2016b; Hearst, 2006; Chuang et al., 2012).

To address these limitations, Selenite leverages the wealth of world knowledge embedded within LLMs to generate overviews of information spaces that are aimed at being comprehensive and high-quality, eliminating the need for manual effort. In addition, Selenite provides detailed explanations of the generated overview as well as contextualizes it within the original content of the source documents to further enhance user understanding and facilitate navigation.

### 2.3. Eliciting Knowledge from LLMs

Recent advances in LLMs like GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023b), PaLM (Chowdhery et al., 2022), and LLaMa (Touvron et al., 2023) showcase impressive capabilities in answering user questions. These models are trained on large volumes of data, and as a result, their parameters might contain a significant body of factual as well as synthesized knowledge across a wide range of domains (Cohen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2020), In this work, we leverage GPT-4 as a knowledge retriever to retrieve a list of commonly considered aspects given an arbitrary topic, which is used to directly help users understand that topic at the beginning and systematically read about and explore that topic afterwards.
However, LLMs face well-known challenges like hallucination and falsehood (Thorp, 2023; Bang et al., 2023; Terry et al., 2023), which could make their outputs uncertain and less trustworthy, often requiring manual inspection and verification before use (Liu et al., 2023a; Terry et al., 2023; Ferdowsi et al., 2023; Gordon et al., 2023). In this work, we address these issues with a two-prong approach: 1) reducing hallucination through techniques such as Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023);
2) grounding LLM generations with the content that users would actually read, enabling natural verification.
